1 — their item 9

Read this first

One requirement is not fully met. Qtech item 7.9 asks for an operator-visible alert when a call fails after its final retry, so counter staff know the display may be stale. The bridge detects the failure, logs it, records it, and exposes it as a health state — but the Nexus screen badge that would put it in front of staff has not been built. That is a change to Nexus, not to this component. It should close before Phase 5 sign-off. Everything else is met.

1Purpose and scope

MWO–OWWA Singapore runs its queue in Nexus, an internal case-management system on the office LAN. The public-facing queue display is supplied and operated by Qtech, hosted on AWS in Singapore. The two systems have no direct connection and are not on the same network.

This bridge closes that gap. When a member of staff calls a number to a counter in Nexus, the bridge forwards that call to Qtech, and the number appears on the wall with a chime and a voice announcement.

In scope

Exactly the two endpoints the Qtech integration response defines, and nothing else:

  • POST /call — sent whenever a number is called to a counter, including a deliberate recall.
  • GET /health — a periodic liveness probe.

Explicitly out of scope

Call is the only event the Qtech interface accepts. There is no missed state, no completed state, no instruction to clear a counter, and no control over how anything is rendered. Accordingly:

  • Missed, deferred, re-instated and served states are not transmitted. They remain inside Nexus, visible to staff only.
  • Nexus does not control the display. Layout, fonts, colours, chime and voice are entirely Qtech’s, configured on their side.
  • No queue features are ported. Estimated waiting time, missed lists and counter status stay internal.

Consequence worth agreeing explicitly

Because there is no clear instruction, a number remains on screen against its counter until that counter calls someone else — including after the client has been served, and overnight after close of business. Whatever is showing at 18:00 is still showing the next morning until the first call. This is Qtech’s documented behaviour, not a defect, but it differs from how the previous display behaved and MWO should confirm it is acceptable.

2Architecture

Two networks, not one

Nexus sits on the MWO office LAN. The bridge, the Qtech equipment and the display sit on the PE network. These are separate networks with no local path between them, which is why the call travels out to Supabase and back rather than crossing the office directly. Supabase is not an incidental hop — it is the only thing joining the two sides.

The bridge and the Qtech equipment both reach the internet; the machine driving the display does not, and depends only on the Qtech equipment beside it.

Why the bridge does not talk to Nexus

Nexus already publishes queue state to Supabase for its own purposes — that is how the check-in kiosks and queue displays work today. The bridge reads that existing stream. Nothing was added to Nexus, and the bridge holds no credential that would let it reach anything on the MWO network.

Health travels back the same way: the bridge keeps a row current in Supabase, and Nexus reads it. Neither system opens a socket to the other.

What this costs

Two things follow from the split, and neither has a local workaround.

Latency has a budget, and most of it is spent before the bridge sees anything. Qtech’s Phase 2 criterion is a display update within two seconds of a call. Nexus mirrors each call to Supabase on a two-second cycle, which alone accounts for up to two of those seconds; the network legs add a few hundred milliseconds and the bridge itself adds almost nothing. Typical total is around 1.2 seconds and the worst case is around 2.6. This should be measured during the pilot rather than assumed, and if it fails, the place to look is the Nexus mirror cadence rather than the bridge.

Both networks need internet for the display to update. If either the MWO LAN or the PE network loses connectivity, calls stop arriving, and there is no local path to fall back to.

3What is sent

Every call is a single JSON POST. This is the complete payload — there are no other fields, and the delivery tests assert that the key set is exactly this:

POST https://<tenant>.qtechqms.com/api/v1/ops/call
Authorization: Basic <credential>
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "eventId":     "b3f1c2e0-9a44-5c81-b0f2-7d1e4a2c9f30",
  "ticketID":    "4c9a1e77-2b6d-4f80-9e13-8a5c2f0b7d64",
  "branchUUID":  "c761bfe7-...",
  "counterName": "7",
  "queueNo":     "A045",
  "timestamp":   "2026-08-20T01:14:22.000Z"
}

silent: true is added in one situation only, described in §5.

Personal data

None is sent. The Nexus record behind a call carries the client’s name, email address and booking reference; the bridge discards all three at the point of reading and never holds, logs or forwards them. Its log files contain queue numbers, counter numbers and opaque identifiers only.

4Detecting a call

This is the part of the design that carries the most weight, and the part most likely to be misjudged by anyone modifying it later.

Nexus has two separate code paths that both result in a number being called, and they update different fields. A naive implementation watching a single field works for months and then silently drops an entire category of call.

PathUsed byFields it updates
CurrentContract verification, Direct Hire, Agency, AccreditationCall time and counter. Never the call counter or last-called time
LegacyThe OWWA windowAll of them
Legacy recallThe OWWA windowOnly the call count and last-called time — the call time never moves

Watching the call time alone — the obvious choice — would be blind to every OWWA recall. The bridge therefore keys on a composite signature across all four fields. Any change while the row is in a called state is a call. This covers a first call, a recall on either path, and a counter takeover, which is a genuine re-announcement at the new counter.

There is a regression test named for the OWWA recall case specifically, so that a future change which reintroduces the fault fails the build rather than the wall.

Two inputs, deliberately overlapping

The bridge subscribes to live change notifications from Supabase, which is fast and push-based. That subscription can fail in a particularly unhelpful way: the connection stays open and simply stops delivering, with no error raised anywhere.

A second mechanism re-reads the day’s called rows every 15 seconds and compares them against the same record of what has already been sent. It catches anything the live feed dropped. The number of calls it has had to recover is itself the signal that the live feed has quietly died, and is reported in the health row.

5Delivery behaviour

Idempotency, retries and recalls

Qtech suppresses a repeated eventId within a ten-minute window and does not re-announce, which makes a retry safe. That places a requirement on us: a retry must reuse its key, and a deliberate recall must use a fresh one.

Rather than generating a key and storing it, the bridge derives it from the ticket and the call’s signature. Two useful properties follow:

  • A retry produces the same key by construction — including a retry that happens after the process has restarted, which a stored key would not survive.
  • A recall changes the signature, so it derives a different key and Qtech re-announces, exactly as intended.

Retry policy

ConditionAction
Network failure, timeout, HTTP 5xx, HTTP 429Retry — 3 attempts, gaps of 1s then 2s
BRANCH_NOT_FOUND, COUNTER_UNKNOWN, VALIDATION_ERRORNo retry — the outcome will not change
HTTP 401 or 403No retry — recorded as an authentication failure
Client timeout10 seconds

After the final retry the bridge stops and records the failure. Stopping is safe by Qtech’s own design: the next call to that counter supersedes the lost one, and there is no catch-up feed to reconcile against. What must not happen is the failure passing unnoticed — which is what §7 addresses.

Ordering

Calls to the same counter are sent strictly one at a time, in order. Nexus can genuinely produce two calls at one counter within milliseconds, because calling the next client automatically marks the previous one missed. Without this, the two requests race and the wall can settle on the older number. Different counters are unaffected and proceed in parallel.

After a restart

On starting, the bridge records the current state of the queue without sending anything. Without this, a restart at 16:00 would replay every call made since 09:00 and re-announce the lot.

It then re-asserts the most recent call at each counter with silent: true, so the wall shows correct current state without chiming. This is the only situation in which silent is used.

Needs confirmation from Qtech

The silent flag is documented as optional and its purpose is to suppress chime and voice, so this is a supported use of the interface. But the integration response describes no clear or refresh instruction, so restoring display state after a restart is our interpretation rather than something Qtech specified. If they would prefer no traffic at startup, it is disabled with one configuration change and no code change.

Calls that cannot be sent

Nexus permits a number to be called without assigning a counter. Qtech requires a counter name, so such a call cannot be delivered. Rather than sending an invalid request and collecting a VALIDATION_ERROR, the bridge refuses it locally, records the reason, and reflects it in the health state. The same applies to a counter that is not on the agreed list.

7Health monitoring

A queue display fails quietly. If calls stop reaching the wall, nothing on screen changes — the last numbers simply stay there, looking entirely normal, while clients wait for a number that was called ten minutes ago. Staff have no way to tell from the wall itself.

The bridge therefore reports its own state continuously. Nexus reads a single value:

StateMeaningTrigger
OKCalls are reaching the displayEverything below is clear
DegradedRunning, but not delivering reliablyLive feed disconnected, Qtech health probe failing, or three consecutive failed calls
DownAssume nothing is reaching the displayNo heartbeat for 90 seconds

What each failure looks like

FailureHow it surfaces
Pi powered off, process dead, SD card failedThe heartbeat stops advancing → Down after 90 seconds
Live feed silently stops deliveringMarked disconnected, or the recovery counter climbing while it still claims to be connected
Qtech unreachable, or credential rejectedFailure streak with an authentication error code
Qtech answers HTTP 200 with an error in the bodyThe specific code is recorded. This is the quietest failure of all — it succeeds at every level except the one that matters
Numbers called with no counter assignedCounted and reflected in the state, so a systemic process problem cannot hide

The heartbeat is the load-bearing part. Every other failure produces a log line somewhere. A dead Pi produces nothing at all — the only detectable signal is a value that should be changing and has stopped. So it is written unconditionally on a timer, not only when something happens.

A separate append-only log records the outcome of every call attempt, which provides the daily call-volume and error-rate review that Qtech’s pilot phase requires.

The outstanding gap

All of the above is detection and recording. Qtech item 7.9 asks for an operator-visible alert — something that tells counter staff, at the counter, that the display may be stale. The data is available for Nexus to read; the screen element that displays it has not been built. Until it is, a failure is visible to whoever looks at the database, not to the person at the window. This is a Nexus change and should be closed before Phase 5.

8Deployment

The full step-by-step procedure is docs/INSTALL.md in the repository, covering a blank Pi through to a running bridge, with a troubleshooting table. Summary:

  1. Apply the database schema — one SQL file, once per environment.
  2. Prepare the Pi — Raspberry Pi OS Lite, Singapore time zone, Node 20 or newer. No desktop or browser is needed.
  3. Clone and install — one script, which creates the service account, builds, deploys and registers the service.
  4. Configure — six values: two for Supabase, four from Qtech.
  5. Dry run — a mode that does everything except the outbound call, proving the Nexus half works before a display is involved.
  6. Go live and confirm it survives a reboot — the failure that stays invisible until the day the power blinks.

Expect around 30 minutes, most of it waiting for downloads. The service restarts automatically on crash and starts on boot. Updating is a pull, a re-run of the installer, and a restart; the credential file is left untouched.

9Acceptance testing

Qtech’s procedure requires calls driven from the MWO–OWWA system. Driving them through the live queue would mean calling real clients to real counters, so the bridge ships with a command-line tool that sends a single call using the same credentials and the same payload builder, prints the raw request and response for schema checking, and performs no retry.

4 — PilotReadyThe call log supplies the daily volume and error-rate review
5 — AcceptanceOne gapItem 7.9 — the operator-visible alert — is outstanding

Worth demonstrating during Phase 3: stopping the bridge causes the reported state to go Down within 90 seconds. That is the silent-failure detection working, and it is the mechanism that will tell staff the wall has stopped updating.

Automated coverage

52 automated tests plus an end-to-end start-up test that drives the whole pipeline against stub servers. The delivery tests assert the behaviours the integration response makes mandatory: retry only on transient faults, never on business errors, one idempotency key across the retries of a single call, per-counter ordering, and the exact payload key set.
